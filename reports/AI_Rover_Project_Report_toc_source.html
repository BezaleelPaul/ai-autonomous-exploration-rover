ABSTRACT

Small autonomous ground vehicles must see, decide, and stop safely within tight limits on power, memory, and cost. This report documents the design, assembly, software integration, testing, and recovery of a low-cost vision-guided differential-drive rover built around three cooperating units: an ESP32-CAM that streams Wi-Fi video, a laptop that runs YOLO-based detection and the decision engine, and an Arduino UNO that enforces hard real-time motor and obstacle safety. The physical platform uses a 2S 18650 battery pack, an LM2596 buck converter, a TB6612FNG dual H-bridge, two TT gear motors, and an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor.

The report follows the build chronologically from the first component photographs on 3 October 2026 to the final power integration on 26 October 2026. It records the mechanical assembly, the two-tier electronics stack, the wiring and power architecture, the firmware and host software, the control law, and the test campaign. It also documents a battery-terminal short-circuit incident that damaged a small number of components, the root-cause analysis that followed, and the corrective actions: insulated terminals, strain relief, an inline fuse, and an upgrade from the baseline L298N driver to a TB6612FNG MOSFET driver.

Bench evidence includes 311 passing tests, clean static analysis, successful Arduino and ESP32 firmware builds, three live Wokwi simulation runs, and measured detector benchmarks. The shipped YOLO11s configuration achieved recall 0.477, F1 0.601, and mAP@0.5 0.640 on the 128-image COCO128 evaluation set at a 320-pixel inference size. The report separates these measured results from the hardware measurements that remain pending, and it closes with limitations, future work, and the engineering lessons drawn from the short-circuit recovery.

Keywords: autonomous rover; edge intelligence; ESP32-CAM; Arduino UNO; YOLO; differential drive; ultrasonic safety; TB6612FNG; power isolation; failure analysis.

CHAPTER 1

Introduction

Autonomous mobile robotics sits at the intersection of mechanical design, power electronics, real-time control, and computer vision. A useful mobile robot must do more than move: it must build a usable picture of its surroundings, choose an action quickly enough to matter, and stop safely when the picture is incomplete. The difficulty is not any one of these tasks in isolation; it is making them cooperate on a small, low-cost platform.

Conventional small robots often solve the perception problem by tethering the vehicle to a laptop or by streaming video to a remote server. This is convenient for a demonstration, but it introduces wireless latency, a dependency on network coverage, and a failure mode in which the robot keeps moving after the link has died. The alternative is to place all intelligence on the robot, which is attractive but quickly runs into the memory and thermal limits of inexpensive microcontrollers. This project takes a middle path: distribute the work across three units, but make the safety decision local and deterministic.

1.1 Background

A differential-drive robot is the simplest useful mobile platform. Two independently driven wheels on a common axis produce forward motion when they turn together and rotation when they turn at different speeds. The mathematics is well understood, the mechanics are inexpensive, and the platform is robust. The challenge is the perception and decision layer that turns a camera image into a left or right wheel command.

Modern object detectors based on deep convolutional networks, and more recently on transformers, can locate people, bottles, and other objects in a video frame with useful accuracy. However, the accurate models are too large for a microcontroller. A practical student build therefore separates the detector, which runs on a laptop or single-board computer, from the actuator controller, which runs on a microcontroller and must never miss a safety deadline. This separation is the central architectural idea of the present rover.

1.2 Motivation

The motivation for the project came from three observations. First, many undergraduate robotics projects demonstrate motion but do not demonstrate dependable stopping. A robot that can follow a bottle but cannot stop at a wall is not yet a useful robot. Second, the components needed for a credible demonstration are now affordable: an ESP32-CAM, an Arduino UNO, a dual H-bridge, two gear motors, and an ultrasonic sensor cost less than a textbook. Third, the engineering is most convincing when the build itself is documented honestly, including the failures.

The project was therefore framed around three questions. Can a low-cost rover track a visual target using only commodity parts? Can it guarantee a stop when the software, the camera, or the communication link fails? And can the build be documented in enough detail that another student could reproduce it, including the mistakes? The short-circuit incident described in Chapter 11 is part of that commitment to honest documentation.

1.3 The Project in Brief

The rover is a two-wheel differential-drive platform with a front caster. An ESP32-CAM mounted on the upper deck captures video and serves it as a Wi-Fi MJPEG stream. A laptop pulls that stream, runs a YOLO-family detector, computes the horizontal error between the target bounding-box centre and the frame centre, and selects one of five commands: forward, backward, left, right, or stop. The command travels over USB serial to an Arduino UNO. The Arduino validates the character, checks the HC-SR04 distance, checks the communication watchdog, and only then drives the TB6612FNG dual H-bridge. The ESP32 and the Arduino have no data wire between them; the ESP32 is power-only. This avoids a 5 V-to-3.3 V level-shift hazard and keeps the camera noise away from the motor controller.

1.4 Objectives

The project set out to achieve the following objectives:

  1. Design and assemble a stable, repairable differential-drive chassis from acrylic plates, TT gear motors, a caster, and a 2S 18650 battery pack.

  2. Build an isolated power architecture in which the motor rail and the logic rail are separate, with a common star ground and an inline fuse.

  3. Implement a camera pipeline that streams live video from the ESP32-CAM to the host over Wi-Fi without a wired data link to the Arduino.

  4. Implement a YOLO-based detector and a three-zone steering policy that converts a bounding box into forward, left, right, or stop commands.

  5. Implement a deterministic safety layer on the Arduino with a 3 s communication watchdog and a 15 cm forward obstacle cutoff.

  6. Document every build stage with date-stamped photographs and a written engineering narrative.

  7. Measure the detector and system performance, separate measured results from design estimates, and record the limitations honestly.

  8. Analyse the 26 October battery short-circuit incident, identify its root cause, and implement corrective actions that reduce the chance of recurrence.

1.5 Scope and Delimitations

The rover is an indoor, low-speed, single-target demonstrator. It is not a self-driving car, a SLAM platform, or a production robot. The perception stack tracks one target class at a time, the camera is monocular, and there are no wheel encoders in the baseline build. The laptop remains part of the control loop for detection and high-level decisions; the Arduino is the local safety authority. The project does not claim collision-free navigation in cluttered spaces, nor does it claim operation in rain, direct sunlight, or on steep slopes.

Within those limits, the scope includes the complete mechanical build, the power and signal wiring, the ESP32 camera firmware, the Arduino motor and safety firmware, the host-side perception and decision software, a dashboard, a test suite, and the documentation set. The project deliberately excludes custom PCB fabrication, 3D-printed enclosures, and battery-management electronics; these are recorded as future work.

CHAPTER 2

Problem Definition and Requirements

The problem is not simply to make a robot move. The problem is to make a robot behave acceptably when the world, the sensors, and the software are all imperfect. A camera can be blinded by a window; a Wi-Fi link can drop; an ultrasonic sensor can miss a soft or angled obstacle; a motor can stall; a battery terminal can touch a metal chassis. The design must be judged by how it behaves in those cases, not only by how well it follows a target in a clean corridor.

2.1 Problem Statement

Design and build a low-cost, vision-guided differential-drive rover that can track a designated object in indoor conditions and stop safely when an obstacle is detected, when the communication link is lost, or when the perception pipeline becomes stale. The rover must be repairable with hand tools, must run from a rechargeable battery, and must be documented in sufficient detail for another student to reproduce the build.

The design must resolve a fundamental tension. Accurate vision requires computation that is difficult to place on a small microcontroller, while safety requires a controller that is not distracted by vision. The chosen resolution is a distributed architecture: the heavy perception runs on a laptop, the camera streams over Wi-Fi, and a separate microcontroller owns the motor and obstacle safety. The laptop may fail; the rover must still stop.

2.4 Constraints and Assumptions

The design was carried out under the following constraints:

  • The host laptop is part of the perception loop; the rover is not fully standalone.

  • The camera is a single OV2640 sensor with no depth channel; distance is inferred from the bounding-box size and from the ultrasonic sensor.

  • The chassis is acrylic and the electronics are exposed; the build is a bench prototype, not a sealed product.

  • The 2S 18650 pack, buck converter, and motor driver are commodity modules with limited datasheet guarantees.

  • The project is developed in a student laboratory with hand tools and a limited component inventory.

The key assumptions are that the floor is flat and dry, the lighting is between roughly 20 and 2000 lux, the target is visible from the front hemisphere, and the operator keeps the rover within Wi-Fi range. These assumptions are stated explicitly because the test results in Chapter 10 are only valid within them.

CHAPTER 3

Literature Review and Technical Background

This chapter reviews the ideas that shaped the design. It is not an exhaustive survey; it focuses on the four areas that directly influenced the rover: differential-drive locomotion, visual servoing, edge-capable object detection, and safety architectures for small robots.

3.1 Differential-Drive Locomotion

Differential drive is the most common locomotion scheme in low-cost robotics. Two wheels on a common axis are driven independently; a caster or skid supports the third point. The forward velocity and yaw rate are determined by the left and right wheel speeds. The scheme is mechanically simple and allows zero-radius rotation, which is valuable in tight indoor spaces. Its weaknesses are wheel slip, surface dependence, and the absence of a direct heading measurement unless an IMU or encoders are added.

The classic treatment by Siegwart, Nourbakhsh, and Scaramuzza provides the kinematic model used in Chapter 9. For a wheel radius r, wheelbase L, and wheel angular speeds omega_L and omega_R, the linear velocity is v = r(omega_L + omega_R)/2 and the yaw rate is omega = r(omega_R - omega_L)/L. These equations are exact only when the wheels do not slip; in practice the rover uses visual feedback to compensate for the difference between the ideal and the real motion.

3.2 Visual Servoing

Visual servoing uses feedback from a camera to control a robot's motion. In image-based visual servoing, the control error is defined directly in image coordinates, for example the horizontal distance between the target centroid and the image centre. This avoids the need for a calibrated 3D model of the scene and is well suited to a low-cost monocular camera. The price is that depth is not directly observed; the controller must rely on apparent size or on an auxiliary range sensor.

The rover uses a simple image-based policy. The frame is divided into left, centre, and right zones. If the target centroid lies in the left zone, the rover turns left; if it lies in the right zone, it turns right; if it lies in the centre zone, it moves forward. A deadband around the centre reduces oscillation. The policy is deliberately coarse, because a coarse policy is easier to test and safer than a finely tuned controller that chatters when the detector is noisy.

3.3 Object Detection on the Edge

The YOLO family, introduced by Redmon et al., reframed object detection as a single regression problem and made real-time detection practical on commodity hardware. Later YOLO versions improved accuracy and efficiency through better backbones, feature pyramids, and training strategies. Transformer-based detectors such as DETR and RT-DETR removed the need for hand-designed anchors and non-maximum suppression, but their computational cost is higher, especially on CPU-only hosts.

For a microcontroller, even a small YOLO model is too large to run comfortably. The TinyML community addresses this with quantization, pruning, and specialised architectures such as FOMO, but these methods require a training pipeline and a carefully curated dataset. This project takes a pragmatic approach: the detector runs on the host laptop, where a full YOLO model can be executed at a usable frame rate, while the rover keeps only the safety-critical logic on the microcontroller. The trade-off is accepted because the project is a prototype, not a product.

The detector comparison in Chapter 10 is important because it shows that the largest or most accurate model is not automatically the right choice. RT-DETRv2-R18 achieved the highest recall and mAP, but its median latency was 3.4 s per frame, which breaks the 1 s control budget. The selected YOLO11s model was the most accurate model that still closed the loop.

3.4 Motor Drivers and Power Electronics

A DC gear motor cannot be driven directly from a microcontroller pin. An H-bridge is required to reverse the polarity and to modulate the average voltage with PWM. The L298N is a widely used bipolar H-bridge that is inexpensive and tolerant, but its internal Darlington output stage drops roughly 1.8 V to 3.2 V, which turns into heat and reduces the voltage available to the motor. MOSFET-based drivers such as the TB6612FNG have a much lower on-resistance and run cooler.

The original design used the L298N because it was available in the laboratory and because its behaviour is well documented. After the 26 October incident, the rover was rebuilt with a TB6612FNG. The change reduced the driver's voltage drop and thermal load, and it made the wiring more compact. The trade-off is that the TB6612FNG has a lower absolute maximum voltage and requires a logic supply and a standby pin, so the wiring and firmware pin map had to be revised.

3.5 Safety Architectures for Small Robots

Safety in small robots is often treated as a software feature: a check inside the main loop. A more robust approach is to give safety its own layer with the authority to override every other command. The rover follows this principle. The Arduino firmware checks the obstacle distance and the communication watchdog before it applies any motor command. The host software also checks the camera health and the serial link, but the host is not trusted to be the last line of defence.

This layering is inspired by the fail-safe principle used in industrial control: the absence of a valid signal must produce a safe state, not a continuation of the last command. The rover's safe state is STOP, with the motor driver disabled. The same principle appears in the FMEA in Chapter 9 and in the incident analysis in Chapter 11.

3.6 Gap Addressed by This Project

The literature offers excellent treatments of each individual subsystem, but relatively few accessible accounts show how the pieces are integrated on a student budget, including the wiring, the power isolation, the test evidence, and the failures. This project contributes a complete, reproducible build narrative: a photo-dated record of the mechanical assembly, a measured detector comparison, a layered safety design, and an honest case study of a battery short circuit and its recovery. The contribution is therefore as much methodological as technical.

The second tier is the host laptop, which runs the detector, the decision engine, the safety manager, the dashboard, and the logger. 4.2 End-to-End Data Flow

Every frame follows the same five-step loop, repeated roughly five to ten times per second depending on the host load. The steps are SEE, UNDERSTAND, DECIDE, ACT, and PROTECT. The loop is intentionally simple so that each step can be tested in isolation and so that a failure at any step has a defined safe outcome.

  1. SEE: the ESP32-CAM captures an OV2640 frame and serves it as MJPEG. The host pulls the stream and stores the latest frame.

  2. UNDERSTAND: the detector returns a bounding box for the target class. The box centre is compared with the frame centre to produce a horizontal error.

  3. DECIDE: the steering policy maps the error to a zone and selects F, L, R, or S. The safety manager can veto the decision.

  4. ACT: the approved one-character command is sent over USB serial to the Arduino.

  5. PROTECT: the Arduino re-checks the command, the obstacle distance, and the watchdog before it enables the motor driver.

4.3 Hardware Interconnection

The hardware interconnection is deliberately sparse. The ESP32-CAM receives 5 V and ground from the buck converter and nothing else. Video leaves it as Wi-Fi MJPEG. The laptop connects to the Arduino with a USB cable, which carries both the command stream and the telemetry. The Arduino connects to the TB6612FNG with six control lines (PWMA, AIN1, AIN2, BIN1, BIN2, PWMB) plus STBY, and to the HC-SR04 with TRIG and ECHO. The motor driver connects to the two TT motors and to the 7.4 V battery rail. All grounds meet at a single star point.

The absence of a data wire between the ESP32 and the Arduino is a deliberate safety decision. The ESP32 is a 3.3 V device, while the Arduino is a 5 V device. A direct UART connection would expose the ESP32 to 5 V on its GPIO pins, which can destroy the pin or the chip. A level shifter could solve the electrical problem, but it would add another component and another failure point. Since the laptop already has a USB link to the Arduino, the ESP32 does not need a wired data path. The design therefore keeps the ESP32 power-only.

Why the ESP32 is power-only

The ESP32-CAM has no data wire to the Arduino. Video travels over Wi-Fi to the laptop, and commands travel from the laptop to the Arduino over USB serial. This avoids a 5 V-to-3.3 V level-shift hazard and keeps the camera's power noise away from the motor controller. If a wired UART is ever added, a level shifter is mandatory.

4.4 Design Rationale

The architecture is a response to three constraints: compute, safety, and cost. Compute is concentrated on the laptop because that is where a full YOLO model can run. Safety is concentrated on the Arduino because a small, single-purpose firmware is easier to audit than a large Python process. Cost is kept low by using commodity modules and by avoiding a single-board computer on the rover. The result is not the most compact design possible, but it is one of the easiest to understand, test, and repair.

The split also makes the failure modes easier to reason about. If the Wi-Fi link drops, the host sees a stale frame and sends STOP; if the host itself dies, the Arduino watchdog stops the motors; if the Arduino loses power, the motor driver is disabled. There is no state in which silence means “keep driving”. This property is the most important architectural achievement of the project.

The vision tier uses an AI-Thinker ESP32-CAM with an OV2640 sensor. The module combines a 240 MHz dual-core ESP32, 520 KB of SRAM, 4 MB of flash, a microSD slot, and a 2 MP camera on a small PCB. 5.3 Motion and Actuation

The drivetrain uses two yellow TT DC gear motors. Each motor integrates a plastic gearbox with a high reduction ratio, which trades speed for torque and makes the rover controllable at low PWM values. The motors are inexpensive, widely available, and mechanically compatible with the acrylic chassis and the rubber tread wheels. Their weaknesses are plastic gears, no encoders, and a wide unit-to-unit variation in speed for the same PWM command.

The baseline motor driver was an L298N dual H-bridge. It is robust and forgiving, and it was available in the laboratory. However, its Darlington output stage drops a significant voltage, which reduces the motor speed and produces heat. After the 26 October incident, the rover was rebuilt with a TB6612FNG MOSFET driver, which has a much lower voltage drop and runs cooler. Both drivers were photographed so the change is documented rather than hidden.

5.4 Power

The rover is powered by a 2S 18650 lithium-ion pack with a nominal voltage of 7.4 V. The pack feeds the motor driver directly and feeds an LM2596 buck converter, which produces a regulated 5 V rail for the Arduino, the ESP32-CAM, and the ultrasonic sensor. The LM2596 is a switching regulator, so it is more efficient than a linear regulator, but it requires careful adjustment before connection. The output voltage must be set to 5.0 V with a multimeter before any logic board is attached.

Set the buck before you connect it

The LM2596 output is adjusted with a small potentiometer. Connect the battery to the input, measure the output with a multimeter, and turn the potentiometer until the output reads 5.0 V. Only then connect the Arduino and the ESP32-CAM. A mis-set buck can destroy both boards in less than a second.

5.5 Sensing

The HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor provides a short-range forward obstacle gate. It measures distance by timing the echo of a 40 kHz burst, and it interfaces with two digital pins (TRIG and ECHO). It is cheap, simple, and adequate for a 15 cm stop threshold. It is not a precision instrument: its cone is narrow, it can miss soft or angled surfaces, and its readings become unreliable beyond about 2 m. The rover uses it as a safety gate, not as a mapping sensor.

5.6 Control

The Arduino UNO R3 is the safety supervisor. It was chosen because it is ubiquitous, 5 V tolerant, easy to audit, and supported by a mature toolchain. It has enough digital pins for the motor driver, the ultrasonic sensor, and a serial link, and its analog inputs are available for future current sensing. Its limitations are a 16 MHz clock, 2 KB of SRAM, and no floating-point accelerator, but none of these matter for the small safety firmware.

Power Architecture and Wiring

Power is the part of a small robot that is easiest to underestimate. A rover can appear to work on the bench and then reset violently the moment both motors start, because the motor current collapses the logic rail. The rover avoids this by keeping the motor rail and the logic rail separate, by using a switching buck converter for the logic, and by bringing every ground back to a single star point. This chapter explains the electrical chain and the reasoning behind each connection.

7.1 Power Rails

There are two electrical domains on the rover. The high-current domain is the 2S battery rail, which ranges from about 6.0 V (discharged) to 8.4 V (fully charged) and feeds the motor driver directly. The low-current domain is the regulated 5 V rail, which feeds the Arduino, the ESP32-CAM, and the HC-SR04. The two domains share a common ground but not a common supply. This separation is the single most important electrical decision in the build.

7.2 Why a Star Ground

A star ground is a wiring topology in which every module's ground returns to one physical point rather than daisy-chaining through other modules. The reason is current. The motor return current can be several hundred milliamps, and the resistance of a long jumper wire is not zero. If the Arduino ground is connected downstream of the motor ground, the motor current produces a small voltage drop in the shared wire, and the Arduino sees a ground that is not at 0 V. That offset can corrupt analog readings, cause serial errors, or reset the board.

The rover uses a single star point on the lower chassis plate. The battery negative, the buck converter input and output grounds, the Arduino ground, the ESP32 ground, the HC-SR04 ground, and the motor driver ground all meet there. The star point is a physical terminal, not a loose twist of wires. This makes the ground inspectable and makes it obvious if a connection has come loose.

Ground rule

Every ground returns to one star point. Never daisy-chain the Arduino ground through the motor driver. A shared ground wire carrying motor current will lift the logic ground and cause resets that look like software bugs.

7.3 Fusing and Switching

The positive battery lead passes through a 3 A inline fuse before the rocker switch. The fuse is sized above the normal operating current but below the current that the battery and wiring can safely deliver into a short. The switch isolates the entire system for assembly and for emergencies. The fuse holder is deliberately placed close to the battery so that the unprotected length of positive conductor is as short as possible.

The short-circuit incident showed why the physical arrangement matters as much as the schematic. A fuse that is present but bypassed by a loose terminal does not protect anything. The corrective action therefore combined the fuse with an insulated terminal, a strain-relieved holder, and a pre-power checklist. Protection is a system, not a single component.

7.6 Connector and Harness Practice

The harness uses Dupont connectors for logic signals and soldered joints for motor and battery leads.   • Strain-relieve every wire that crosses a moving part.

  • Separate motor leads from signal leads; cross at right angles when unavoidable.

  • Label both ends of every non-obvious wire before it disappears into the chassis.

  • Photograph the harness before closing the deck; the photograph is the maintenance manual.

CHAPTER 8

Software and AI Pipeline

The software is split along the same three tiers as the hardware. The ESP32-CAM runs a small camera firmware. The laptop runs the perception, decision, dashboard, and logging stack. The Arduino runs a compact safety and motor firmware. The interfaces between them are deliberately narrow: MJPEG over Wi-Fi and one-character commands over USB serial. Narrow interfaces are easier to test and easier to reason about when something fails.

8.1 Host Software Architecture

The host is a Python application organised into layers. The capture layer pulls frames from the ESP32 stream or from a file. The perception layer runs the detector and returns bounding boxes. The decision layer converts the selected box into a command. The safety layer checks confidence, distance, and link health before the command is sent. The transport layer writes the command to the serial port. The presentation layer draws the dashboard and writes the event log.

The layers communicate through plain data structures rather than through shared mutable state. A frame becomes a detection result; a detection result becomes a decision; a decision becomes a serial command. This makes it possible to replay a recorded session through the decision and safety layers without a camera, which is how most of the regression tests are run.

8.2 Detection Model

The detector is a YOLO11s model fine-tuned for the target class used in the demonstration. The model was selected after a comparison of five candidates on the same 128-image COCO128 evaluation set. The comparison is reported in Chapter 10. The model runs at a 320-pixel inference size, which is a compromise between accuracy and latency on the 2-core, 4-thread host CPU.

The detector returns boxes with class labels and confidence scores. The decision layer keeps the highest-confidence box for the configured target class and discards boxes below a confidence threshold. A temporal filter requires the target to be present for a small number of consecutive frames before the rover is allowed to move, which reduces spurious starts caused by a single false positive.

8.3 Decision Engine

The decision engine is intentionally simple. It computes the horizontal offset between the target box centre and the frame centre, normalises the offset by the frame width, and compares it with two thresholds. If the offset is within the centre deadband, the command is FORWARD. If the target is to the left, the command is LEFT. If it is to the right, the command is RIGHT. If no target is present, the command is STOP.

The deadband is the most important tuning parameter. A narrow deadband makes the rover track aggressively but oscillate when the detector jitters. A wide deadband makes the rover stable but slow to correct. The chosen value is a compromise, and it is stored in configuration rather than hard-coded so that it can be adjusted without editing the application logic.

8.4 Communication Protocol

The host-to-Arduino protocol is deliberately minimal. The host sends one ASCII character per command: `F` for forward, `B` for backward, `L` for left, `R` for right, and `S` for stop. The Arduino replies with a short status line. Unknown characters produce `STATUS:ERROR` and do not actuate the motors. The protocol is small enough to be implemented correctly on an 8-bit microcontroller and simple enough to be inspected on a serial terminal.

8.5 ESP32-CAM Firmware

The ESP32-CAM firmware configures the OV2640, starts a Wi-Fi access point or joins an existing network, and serves the MJPEG stream on port 81. It also exposes a small control endpoint for changing resolution and frame quality. The firmware is kept minimal on purpose: every additional feature is another way for the camera to fail, and the camera is the least reliable part of the system.

The camera board has no USB-serial bridge, so flashing requires either a programming baseboard or a manual jumper sequence that pulls GPIO0 low at reset. The baseboard is the safer option because it exposes the correct pins and avoids accidental shorts. The firmware is flashed at 115200 baud and verified by opening the stream in a browser before the board is installed on the rover.

8.6 Arduino Firmware

The Arduino firmware is a small finite-state machine. It reads the serial port, validates the command, reads the ultrasonic sensor, checks the watchdog, and drives the motor driver. The loop is non-blocking: the ultrasonic measurement uses a timeout rather than a long delay, and the serial read returns immediately when no byte is available. This keeps the watchdog responsive even when the sensor is not returning an echo.

The firmware treats every input as untrusted. A command is accepted only if it is one of the five valid characters. A distance is accepted only if it is within the sensor's usable range. A communication link is considered alive only if a valid command has arrived within the last 3 seconds. If any check fails, the motors are stopped and the driver is disabled.

8.7 Logging and Reproducibility

Every run writes a CSV event log with the timestamp, frame index, detection count, selected class, confidence, bounding-box centre, distance, command, safety state, and inference time. The log is the basis for the evaluation in Chapter 10. It also makes it possible to replay a run through the decision layer and confirm that the same inputs produce the same outputs.

Configuration is stored in YAML rather than in code. The camera URL, model path, confidence threshold, deadband, serial port, and safety limits are all configurable. This matters for reproducibility: a test can be repeated with the exact configuration that produced the recorded result, and a change can be reviewed as a diff rather than as an unexplained edit.

CHAPTER 9

Control Law and Safety Architecture

The control law turns a bounding box into wheel motion. The safety architecture decides whether that motion is allowed. The two are deliberately separated: the control law may be tuned for smooth tracking, while the safety layer applies hard limits that no tuning can override. This chapter describes both, and it records the failure modes that the design is intended to survive.

9.1 Differential-Drive Kinematics

For a differential-drive robot with wheel radius r, wheelbase L, and wheel angular speeds omega_L and omega_R, the linear velocity is v = r(omega_L + omega_R)/2 and the yaw rate is omega = r(omega_R - omega_L)/L. The rover does not measure wheel speed directly in the baseline build, so the firmware approximates the commanded velocity with a PWM duty cycle. The approximation is adequate for a low-speed demonstrator, but it is not a substitute for encoders.

The kinematic model explains why the rover turns in place when the wheels are driven in opposite directions and why it moves straight when they are driven at equal speeds. It also explains the main mechanical weakness: if the two motors are not matched, the rover curves even when the commanded speeds are equal. The visual feedback loop compensates for this by adjusting the command based on the observed target position.

9.2 Steering Law

The steering law is a proportional controller with a deadband. The error e is the normalised horizontal offset between the target centre and the frame centre. The command is LEFT when e is below the negative threshold, RIGHT when e is above the positive threshold, and FORWARD when e lies inside the deadband. The output is discrete because the serial protocol uses single characters, but the underlying control idea is continuous.

A proportional gain Kp scales the turn intensity. A high gain produces a fast response but can overshoot and oscillate; a low gain is stable but sluggish. The comparison in the figure below shows the trade-off for three gains. The selected gain is the middle value, which settles without sustained oscillation on the bench.

9.3 Finite-State Machine

The rover's behaviour is organised as a finite-state machine with five states: IDLE, SEARCH, TRACK, AVOID, and STOP. The machine starts in IDLE, enters SEARCH when a mission begins, enters TRACK when a target is confirmed, enters AVOID when the ultrasonic sensor reports a close obstacle, and enters STOP on a communication loss, a camera failure, or an explicit stop command. STOP is the only state that disables the motor driver.

9.4 Safety Hierarchy

Safety is implemented as a hierarchy of checks, each with the authority to stop the rover. The first check is in the host: if the camera frame is stale, the host sends STOP. The second check is in the serial transport: if the Arduino does not receive a valid command within 3 seconds, it stops the motors. The third check is in the Arduino loop: if the ultrasonic distance is below 15 cm, forward motion is refused. The fourth check is electrical: if the Arduino loses power, the motor driver's standby pin goes low and the outputs are disabled.

9.5 Watchdog Design

The watchdog is a software timer in the Arduino firmware. Every valid command resets the timer. If the timer reaches 3 seconds, the firmware calls the stop routine, sets the state to STOP, and disables the motor driver. The watchdog is not a hardware watchdog timer; it is a communication timeout. It protects against a dead host, a pulled USB cable, or a serial driver that has stopped writing. It does not protect against a firmware hang, which is why the loop is kept short and non-blocking.

The safe state is STOP

Silence must never mean “keep driving”. If the host stops sending, the Arduino stops the motors after 3 seconds. If the camera stops streaming, the host sends STOP. If the Arduino loses power, the driver is disabled. Every failure path converges on the same safe state.

9.7 Test Plan

The test plan follows a pyramid. At the base are unit tests for the decision, safety, and protocol logic. In the middle are integration tests that feed recorded frames and synthetic sensor values through the host stack. At the top are end-to-end tests that run the firmware in simulation and, where possible, on the physical rover. The measured results are reported in Chapter 10.

  • Unit: threshold boundaries, deadband edges, command parsing, watchdog expiry, distance validation, and log schema.

  • Integration: recorded frame sequences replayed through the detector, decision engine, and serial transport with a mock Arduino.

  • Simulation: Wokwi runs of the Arduino firmware with synthetic ultrasonic distances and serial commands.

  • Hardware: continuity, voltage, motor direction, obstacle stop, and link-loss stop.

  • Regression: the full suite is run before every commit that touches the decision or safety layers.

CHAPTER 11

Case Study: The 26 October Battery Short Circuit

On 26 October 2026, at approximately 20:14, the rover suffered a battery short circuit during power integration. The positive terminal of the 2S pack touched the grounded aluminium chassis while the pack was connected. The event damaged the terminal lug and the compression spring, and it affected a small number of components on the power path. No one was injured. This chapter reconstructs the event, identifies the root cause, describes the damage, and records the corrective actions that changed the design.

Incident at a glance

Date: 26 October 2026, approximately 20:14. Location: electronics bench. Trigger: positive battery terminal contacted the grounded chassis. Immediate effect: short circuit, terminal and spring damage, power-path components affected. Immediate action: power removed, pack isolated, bench cleared. Root cause: exposed positive terminal combined with a grounded chassis and no strain relief.

11.1 What Happened

The rover had been running on the bench with the L298N driver. The TB6612FNG had just been photographed and was being prepared for installation. The battery pack was seated in its holder, and the positive lead was being positioned for a continuity check. The terminal lug was exposed; the chassis plate beneath it was grounded through the motor mounts and the common ground. When the lug touched the plate, the pack was shorted through the chassis.

The short was brief but energetic. The terminal lug and the compression spring were damaged by the current and the heat. The pack was removed immediately, and the bench was cleared of loose metal. The rover was not powered again that evening. The damaged parts were photographed the same night, and the photograph is included in this report as primary evidence.

11.3 Root Cause

The immediate cause was a positive terminal in contact with a grounded conductor. The underlying causes were more interesting. First, the terminal lug was exposed: the soldered joint had been checked electrically but not insulated mechanically. Second, the battery holder allowed the pack to shift, so the lead could move into contact with the chassis. Third, the positive lead had no strain relief, so the terminal was loaded by the weight of the wire. Fourth, the bench procedure did not require the pack to be disconnected before the lead was repositioned.

This is a classic Swiss-cheese failure: several small weaknesses lined up. Any one of them would have prevented the event. If the lug had been insulated, the contact would have been harmless. If the holder had restrained the pack, the lead would not have moved. If the lead had been strain-relieved, the terminal would not have been loaded. If the procedure had required disconnection, the pack would not have been live during the adjustment.

The incident is documented because it happened, not because it is unusual. Small robots are often built with exposed terminals and live adjustments. The value of the case study is in the corrective actions, not in the drama of the event.

11.5 Corrective Actions

The rebuild addressed both the design and the procedure. On the design side, the positive terminal was covered with heat-shrink, the battery holder was fitted with a retention bracket and foam, the lead was given a service loop and a cable tie, and a 3 A inline fuse was added close to the pack. The L298N was replaced with a TB6612FNG, which reduced the thermal load and the voltage drop. On the procedure side, a pre-power checklist was introduced and the pack is now disconnected before any lead is repositioned.

  1. Insulate the positive terminal with heat-shrink and a snap-on cover.

  2. Add a retention bracket and foam so the pack cannot shift in the holder.

  3. Provide a service loop and a cable tie for strain relief.

  4. Fit a 3 A inline fuse within 10 cm of the positive terminal.

  5. Replace the L298N with the TB6612FNG and verify the new pin map.

  6. Disconnect the pack before repositioning any lead.

  7. Complete the pre-power checklist before every power-up.

  8. Photograph the power path after every change.

11.7 Lessons Learned

The incident changed the way the project thinks about safety. The first lesson is that a fuse is not a substitute for insulation; it limits the consequence of a fault but does not prevent the fault. The second is that mechanical retention is part of electrical safety: a battery that can move is a battery that can short. The third is that procedures are engineering artefacts; a checklist is as important as a capacitor. The fourth is that the cheapest driver is not always the best driver: the TB6612FNG upgrade reduced heat and voltage drop, and it made the power path easier to inspect.

The final lesson is about documentation. The damaged terminal was photographed immediately, before it was repaired. That photograph is now the most useful image in the report, because it makes the failure concrete. A report that showed only the finished rover would be less useful to the next student than a report that shows what went wrong and why.

CHAPTER 12

Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work

The rover meets its core objectives: it streams video, detects a target, steers toward it, stops at an obstacle, and stops when the link is lost. It does so with commodity parts and a documented build. It is not a finished product, and this chapter is explicit about the gap between the prototype and a dependable autonomous system.

12.1 What Worked

  • The three-tier split kept vision and safety independent. A host failure does not prevent the Arduino from stopping the motors.

  • The ESP32-CAM Wi-Fi stream was reliable at short range and required no data wiring to the Arduino, which avoided a level-shift hazard.

  • The YOLO11s model met the 1 s control budget and produced usable bounding boxes for the target class.

  • The event-log evaluation found zero violations of ten safety invariants across 9,118 rows.

  • The photo-dated build log made it possible to reconstruct the project honestly, including the incident.

12.2 Limitations

12.3 Threats to Validity

The detector results are valid for the COCO128 subset and the chosen confidence threshold. They are not a claim about performance on a different dataset, in low light, or with a different camera. The event-log results are valid for the recorded runs, which were mostly bench sessions with a short USB cable. The safety statistics therefore under-represent link-loss scenarios. The hardware results are the weakest evidence: the final driver build has not completed the runtime, current, and stopping-distance tests.

The build narrative is based on photograph timestamps, which record when the image was taken, not necessarily when the work was done. A photograph taken at 21:36 may show a session that began hours earlier. The narrative uses the timestamps as anchors and avoids claiming more precision than the metadata supports.

12.4 Future Work

The next version of the rover should address the following, in priority order:

  1. Complete the hardware test campaign for the TB6612FNG build: runtime, current, driver temperature, stopping distance, and ten-trial obstacle repeatability.

  2. Add quadrature encoders and a closed-loop wheel-speed controller to reduce curvature and enable odometry.

  3. Move detection onto an on-board single-board computer or edge accelerator so the rover does not depend on a laptop.

  4. Add a hardware watchdog timer and a power-on self-test that checks the driver, the sensor, and the serial link.

  5. Design a 3D-printed enclosure with a fused, keyed battery connector and a serviceable cable harness.

  6. Add a second ranging sensor or a LiDAR to reduce the blind spots of the HC-SR04.

  7. Collect a domain-specific dataset and fine-tune the detector for indoor lighting and the actual target objects.

  8. Implement multi-target tracking and a simple occupancy map for cluttered spaces.

12.5 Broader Reflection

The project's most valuable outcome is not the rover itself. It is the demonstration that a small, distributed system can be made safer by assigning authority explicitly: the laptop is allowed to perceive and decide, but it is not allowed to be the last line of defence. The same pattern appears in industrial control, in automotive systems, and in any robot that must fail safely. The rover is a small example of a large idea.

The second outcome is methodological. The photo-dated log, the measured benchmarks, and the incident case study form a record that another student can follow. That record is more useful than a polished demonstration video, because it shows the decisions and the mistakes that a video would hide.

CHAPTER 13

Conclusion

This report documented the design, assembly, software integration, testing, and recovery of a low-cost vision-guided differential-drive rover. The rover uses an ESP32-CAM for Wi-Fi video, a laptop for YOLO-based detection and decision-making, and an Arduino UNO for deterministic motor and obstacle safety. The three tiers are connected by narrow interfaces: MJPEG over Wi-Fi and one-character commands over USB serial.

The build was completed in stages between 3 and 26 October 2026. The mechanical base, the ultrasonic mount, the bench wiring, the two-tier stack, the Wi-Fi stream, and the insulated motor harness were each photographed and recorded. The final power integration was interrupted by a battery short circuit that damaged the terminal and a small number of power-path components. The recovery added insulation, a fuse, strain relief, a pre-power checklist, and a TB6612FNG driver upgrade.

The measured evidence supports three conclusions. First, a commodity ESP32-CAM and a laptop can provide usable visual servoing for a small indoor rover. Second, a YOLO11s detector at 320 pixels can meet a 1 s control budget on a modest CPU while achieving recall 0.477, F1 0.601, and mAP@0.5 0.640 on the COCO128 evaluation set. Third, a layered safety architecture with a 3 s communication watchdog and a 15 cm obstacle gate can enforce a safe stop even when the perception tier is unreliable.

The project also produced a methodological result: a photo-dated build narrative, a measured benchmark, and an honest incident case study are more valuable to the next student than a polished but undocumented demonstration. The rover is a prototype, not a product. Its limitations are known, its pending measurements are labelled, and its next steps are specific. That is the appropriate state for a student project at this stage.

Final statement

The rover can see, decide, and stop. The safety layer is independent of the vision layer, the failure paths converge on STOP, and the build is documented well enough to be reproduced and improved.
